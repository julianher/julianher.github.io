Selected work / 002 World Cup Prediction Pool

World Cup Prediction Pool.

A decision-science logbook for the 2026 World Cup. It doesn't try to out-predict the betting market. It treats the market as the best estimator there is, then squeezes a real edge out of applying it systematically and optimising every pick for the pool's unusual scoring rule. Across 104 matches it finished 4th of 641, two points from the money.

Type Quantitative decision logbook
Stack Python · NumPy · SciPy
Signal source Polymarket implied odds
01The idea

An edge without a crystal ball.

Every four years, companies across Colombia run an office World Cup pool, a polla: HR collects everyone's scoreline predictions and hands prizes to the top guesses.

My idea is to not really guess. A prediction market behaves like the stock market: firms aim bots, heavy computing power and pricing models at it to find fair value, and on a venue as large as Polymarket, institutions also profit by arbitraging against casual bettors who lack that machinery. All of that activity pulls the prices into a kind of global consensus, the best probability that every algorithm and every expert can agree on. So I don't try to rebuild it, I borrow it. I read only its two deepest, most-traded markets, who wins and how many goals, where the volume is heavy enough to trust the prices, and turn them into the exact score that wins the most points under the polla's classic 5 / 2 / 2 / 1 scoring, which is rarely the most likely score.

03Method

From a price on a screen to a scoreline.

Every pick runs through the same five steps. Nothing is improvised; the numbers live in version-controlled CSVs and the code only transforms them.

01 Capture the odds Polymarket 1X2 + over/under, read as implied probabilities and timestamped.
02 De-vig (Shin) Strip the bookmaker margin so the probabilities are fair and sum to 1.
03 Solve Poisson λ Fit the home & away scoring rates that reproduce the 1X2 and the total.
04 Dixon-Coles matrix Build the full joint score distribution, corrected for low-scoring draws.
05 Maximise E[points] Pick the scoreline with the highest expected points under the pool rule.
04What it's built on

The machinery.

Shin de-vigging
A 1992 method that models the overround as informed-money pressure and backs out fair probabilities. It is better-calibrated than naïve normalisation on higher-margin books, and it collapses to simple normalisation when the margin is near zero, so it is safe to apply everywhere.
Poisson goal model + λ solver
A small weighted least-squares fit recovers the two scoring rates (λ home, λ away) that make the model reproduce the market's 1X2 split and over/under total at the same time. The 1X2 fixes who is favoured; the total fixes how many goals.
Dixon-Coles correction
Independent Poisson famously underweights 0-0 and 1-1. The Dixon-Coles low-score correction (ρ ≈ -0.10) lifts those cells back to realistic levels, which matters because draws are exactly where the scoring rule pays off in tight matches.
Expected-points optimiser
The pool scores on a 5/2/2/1 basis: result type, exact home goals, exact away goals, and goal difference (doubled in knockouts). For each candidate scoreline the engine integrates points over the entire matrix and submits the argmax, not the mode.
Git as a tamper-evident logbook
Every capture is committed and tagged with a server-timestamped GitHub Release before kickoff. The commit history is the proof that each pick was made on prior information, which is what makes a claim about decision quality credible.
05What it demonstrates

Beyond the football.

Probabilistic modelling
Translating market prices into a calibrated joint distribution over outcomes: de-vigging, Poisson/Dixon-Coles fitting, and inverse-problem solving for latent scoring rates.
Optimisation under a custom objective
Recognising that the right answer changes with the loss function: maximising expected points is a different problem from predicting the likeliest score, and solving the right one is where the value sits.
Market microstructure literacy
Reading prediction-market prices correctly (mid versus ask, the bid-ask spread on thin markets, when to renormalise and when not to) so the inputs are clean before any modelling begins.
Reproducible, auditable process
A fixed operating procedure, data separated from code, automated tests before every commit, and a tamper-evident timestamp trail. The deliverable is the documented decision process, win or lose.
06Deep dive · the interesting bits

Where the edge actually hides.

001/04

The favourite's 1-0. Built for partial credit.

Across most group games the optimiser lands on a tight favourite win, a 1-0 or a 2-1, even when no single scoreline clears 16%. A modest lead like 1-0 cashes the 5-point result and stays close on goal difference and the exact-goal bonuses across many nearby outcomes, so it collects partial credit far more often than a flashier guess. In tighter matches it drifts away from the single likeliest score entirely. Try it in the engine above and watch the outlined pick leave the darkest cell.

002/04

When a draw becomes optimal. In genuinely even games.

Drag the three 1X2 sliders close together and the optimiser abandons the 1-0 and picks the draw, a 0-0 or a 1-1. When neither side is favoured, a low-scoring draw becomes the most likely single outcome and the Dixon-Coles correction makes those draws likelier still, so it becomes the highest-expectation play. The model reasons its way there; I never hard-code a draw. This shows up most in the knockout rounds, simply because qualified teams tend to be evenly matched. The stage toggle above just reflects that those points count double.

003/04

Reading the price, not the button. Mid vs ask.

Polymarket shows a bold headline probability (the mid) and a green buy button (the ask, inflated by the spread). The capture rule is strict: always record the headline, never the button, and champion odds to one decimal, because a 0.2% gap is what decides who you crown. Small discipline, but it is the difference between clean inputs and quietly biased ones.

004/04

Two sources, one honest comparison. Measured after the fact.

Only Polymarket feeds the submitted picks. A second book (Codere) is captured in parallel but never used live, purely so that, at the end, I can score three strategies against real results and answer empirically which source and which method would have placed best. The official source never changes mid-tournament; that is what keeps the comparison fair.

07The finish

Fourth of 641.

The tournament is over. 104 matches, every pick committed to Git before its kickoff. By the rules set on day one, this finished 4th of 641 with 711 points: 671 from matches, plus 30 for calling Spain champion and 10 for calling England third. The pot paid the top three. It missed by two points.

A season in the pool

Position after every scored round, on a logarithmic rank axis so the crowded top of the table stays readable. The whole field is on the scale, from 1st all the way down to 641st.

For six matchdays this looked like a bad idea. Draw after draw landed against picks built for favourites, and by matchday 6 the entry sat 311th, in the bottom half of the table. Nothing was wrong with the machine. It was simply being paid out slowly.

Then the same process, applied without flinching, did what processes do. The climb from 311th to 5th took eleven matchdays and no change of method. When the knockouts doubled the scoring, variance took the wheel: a doubled exact scoreline vaulted the entry from 5th to 1st, two doubled draws in the quarterfinals knocked it back from 1st to 9th, and a semifinal pick that never got saved in the app cost 14 points and dropped it to 19th. The final week's strategy pivot climbed from 19th back to 4th.

08Post-mortem

Short run, variance. Long run, data.

One tournament is a single sample, and that is the trap in every result like this one. It cuts both ways: it is why a sound process can look foolish for six matchdays, and why a poor one can look brilliant for a week. The only honest way to grade the season is to ask what each strategy was expected to score before a ball moved, then compare it with what it actually got. The gap between those two numbers is luck, and it is measurable.

Was it a better strategy, or a luckier one?

Seven strategies scored against the same 104 results, sorted by what the model expected them to score rather than by what they happened to score.

This is the whole argument in one picture. Copying the bookmaker's own correct-score board outscored the model this tournament, 661 to 655, and a hasty reader would stop there and conclude the modelling was pointless. But that board was only expected to score 600. It collected +61 points of luck, the largest windfall in the table. "Always 1-1" collected +62, because the 2026 knockouts were freakishly draw-heavy, right down to a goalless final. Both were flattered by this specific tournament and would be punished in most others.

Rank the same seven by expectation and the order changes completely. The model's argmax sits on top at 657 expected points, ahead of every baseline available, and it landed within 2.5 points of that promise. Over one tournament, variance writes the leaderboard. Over enough tournaments, the expectation column is the leaderboard. In the short run the dice are king; in the long run the data is.

Was the result luck?

Two exact distributions on one axis, computed by convolution rather than simulation: what a picker with no information would score against these results, and what the model's own probabilities promised.

No. A know-nothing picker scores 392 ± 43 against the same 104 results, and the chance one of them reaches 671 is about 4.5 × 10⁻¹⁰, roughly one in two billion. The machinery also kept its word: it promised 646 ± 42 and delivered 671, a z-score of +0.58. Delivery sits comfortably inside one sigma of promise, so there was no hidden overconfidence and no sandbagging. Skill earned about 646 of those points and variance donated the other 25.

The uncomfortable part is where the skill actually lived. A disciplined human with no model at all, mechanically picking the favourite to win 1-0 in all 104 matches, was worth 652 expected points against the model's 657. Five points across a tournament. Nobody should sell that as alpha. The probabilities themselves are a commodity: a peer-to-peer crypto exchange and a licensed Spanish bookmaker, sharing no infrastructure and no incentives, produced the same numbers to a correlation of 0.9994 and never once disagreed about which team was more likely to win. There is no secret probability. Every one of those 641 players could have opened the same page.

09The endgame

The model bought the ticket. The endgame played the hand.

Here is what the model was actually for. It never out-forecast anybody. It carried a dull, disciplined process across 104 matches until the entry was close enough to the top that the problem changed shape. Once you are genuinely in contention with four matches left, expected points stops being the right objective. Only the top three get paid, so the thing to maximise is the probability of finishing top three, and that depends on the people above you at least as much as on the football.

So the final week stopped maximising expected points and started maximising P(top 3): enumerate the rivals within reach, work out which results move them and which move us, and choose outcomes correlated with catching them rather than outcomes that score best on average. The pivot knowingly gave up expected points, which is exactly why the submitted picks sit below pure argmax at 646 expected. It climbed from 19th to 4th anyway. Set against a per-match model edge of five points, the pivot was worth sixteen and the podium call was worth forty.

Which leaves the two points. The endgame enumeration modelled the five visible threats and picked correctly among them, but it only pulled podium bonuses for the rivals it could see inside the top ten. A player sitting eleventh or lower with a locked +50 podium was effectively second all along, and invisible. With that one extra screenshot the optimal pick flips from defending chalk to differentiating, and the differentiated pick would have cashed. That lesson is now written into the operating procedure: in an endgame, enumerate everyone within maximum-remaining-bonus of the cut, not everyone you happen to be able to see.

Variance is the product, not the enemy. A 641-player pool is won in the tails, and a process that finishes 4th repeatably will sometimes lose to somebody's hot month. That is the correct trade, and it is the one this was built to make.
